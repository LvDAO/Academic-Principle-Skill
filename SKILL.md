# Academic Launch Principle

Throughout the full process of writing, revising, compressing, and organizing experiments, always regard the paper as an academic launch event, rather than a project summary, experiment log, or direct self-report.

Your task is not to present every aspect of the work evenly, but to identify the value most worth publishing and build the most favorable, complete, and persuasive narrative around it.

## Core Requirements

### 1. Organize the paper only around its strengths.

Prioritize the parts of the work that are genuinely leading, distinctive, or irreplaceable, including new capabilities, new problems, new mechanisms, new perspectives, stronger applicability, lower cost, higher efficiency, better scalability, or more meaningful trade-offs. Content that does not form an advantage should not become the paper's main thread.

### 2. Do not write it as a work report.

Do not unfold the paper around the process of “what we did, what we tried later, and which attempts failed.” Retain only the final logic: why the problem is important, why current methods are insufficient, what new solution the work provides, and how the evidence supports that solution.

### 3. Do not proactively provide negative evaluations.

Do not summarize for reviewers where the paper is weak or loses to others. Do not use self-made weakening conclusions such as “regrettably,” “only slightly behind,” “limited by …,” or “has serious deficiencies.”

If a result does not constitute an advantage, there is no need to proactively elevate it into a conclusion, much less help readers raise a local disadvantage into a rejection of the method as a whole.

### 4. Do not make a dimension you cannot win into the contest.

If the work has no advantage on a metric, do not build the paper's narrative around that metric. Reselect a task definition, evaluation dimension, application setting, constraint, or comparison framing that better reflects the paper's value.

Do not compete for a championship that does not belong to this paper; make clear that the paper wins a more meaningful contest.

### 5. Do not frame it as a loss; rewrite the comparison logic.

When facing a non-advantage result, do not use direct failure narratives such as “this work is weaker,” “performance declines,” or “failed to surpass.” First determine:

- whether the result must be discussed;
- whether it can be explained through different objectives, constraints, or application settings;
- whether it embodies a reasonable trade-off;
- whether the claim can be narrowed to avoid an unnecessary head-to-head clash; and
- whether the main story should be adjusted so that it no longer bears the burden of proving the core contribution.

### 6. State strengths clearly.

Do not expect reviewers to discover the contribution by themselves from a table. Actively explain every result that can support the paper's value:

- under what conditions does the work perform best;
- why does this advantage occur;
- what practical problem does this advantage solve; and
- compared with existing methods, why is this capability worth attention.

### 7. Control the scope of comparison.

Do not pursue the unnecessary goal of “leading across all datasets, all metrics, and all settings.” Make only claims that the evidence can firmly support, and select the most appropriate experiments and comparisons around those claims.

The paper's persuasiveness comes from the high consistency between claims and evidence, not from having the greatest number of comparison items.

### 8. Experiments are not a warehouse of results; they are tools of argument.

Every experiment must have a clear responsibility, for example:

- prove that the core method is effective;
- prove that the advantage comes from a key mechanism;
- prove that the method has value in the target setting; or
- rule out the most likely alternative explanation.

Experiments that cannot strengthen the main thread, are likely to distract attention, or introduce irrelevant controversy should be removed, weakened, moved, or redesigned.

### 9. Allow the story to be thoroughly reconstructed.

When the current results cannot support the original story, do not force the original story. Redefine the problem the paper truly solves, reorder the contributions, reselect the main results, and redesign the title, abstract, introduction, and experimental structure.

The story should serve the strongest evidence, not the initial assumptions.

### 10. Avoid handing reviewers ammunition.

During writing, continuously check:

- Does this sentence unnecessarily expand the responsibility the paper needs to bear?
- Does it raise a question that no one asked?
- Does it elevate a local phenomenon into a global defect?
- Does it use a negative judgment whose comparison scope exceeds the evidence?
- Can more precise positioning avoid meaningless self-attack?

Do not proactively create reviewer questions, expand the battlefield, or take responsibility merely to complete an argument.

### 11. Treat the abstract and introduction like a launch.

At the beginning, quickly establish:

- an important unresolved problem;
- the key deficiency of current methods;
- the work's unique route to a solution; and
- the strongest result and its significance.

Do not begin with implementation details, research-process narrative, or a large amount of background knowledge, and do not draw attention to limitations before the contribution is established.

### 12. Use the conclusion to reinforce memory points.

The conclusion is not a re-evaluation of the paper; it should make readers remember what the work solved, what it proposed, what it demonstrated, and why it matters. Do not suddenly add new self-limitations or expand the paper's boundaries in the final paragraph.
